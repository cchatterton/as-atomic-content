Atomic Design — what is it?

Brad Frost's atomic design is a way to think about an interface as a connected system of parts and wholes. It describes five levels: atoms, molecules, organisms, templates, and pages. The levels give a team a shared language for discussing how small interface elements combine into a complete experience.

Atoms

Atoms are the smallest useful interface elements: a button, input, label, icon, or heading style, for example. Each has its own appearance and behaviour. Looking at these basics together reveals whether the interface is consistent.

Molecules

Molecules combine a few atoms into one simple component with a purpose. A search field, its label, and a submit button can form a search control. The parts still matter, but the combination now performs a task.

Organisms

Organisms are larger, distinct sections of an interface assembled from atoms, molecules, or other organisms. A site header might bring together a logo, navigation, and search control. A list of repeated content items can also form an organism.

Templates

Templates arrange those components into a page-level structure. They show where content belongs and what the layout must accommodate, such as image proportions or varying text lengths, without relying on one final set of copy and images.

Pages

Pages put representative real content into a template. This is where the system is tested as people will experience it. A long title, missing image, or unusual amount of content can expose a problem that was not visible when a component was viewed alone.

Use the model in both directions

These levels are a mental model, not a five-step production sequence. You can examine a page, identify the smaller parts that shape it, improve a component, and then return to the page to see whether the change works in context. The parts influence the whole, and real content can reveal what the parts need to do better.
